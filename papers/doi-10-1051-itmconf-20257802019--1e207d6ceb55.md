# **Comparative Analysis of Cnn Architectures for Bird Species Classification**

_Jiayi Tang_

Department of Economics, Lingnan University, HongKong, China

**Abstract.** Avian species identification constitutes a fundamental component in biodiversity conservation, ecological monitoring, and environmental research initiatives. Conventional manual identification approaches frequently demonstrate limitations including extensive time requirements, susceptibility to human error, and difficulties in processing large image collections. The emergence of artificial intelligence frameworks, particularly Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), offers transformative potential for automating avian classification tasks with unprecedented accuracy. This investigation presents a methodical evaluation of three prominent CNN architectural families—Residual Neural Network, Visual Geometry Group, and MobileNet_v2 specifically for bird species recognition applications. Utilizing an extensive dataset comprising 84,635 high-resolution images representing 525 distinct avian species, the research implements sophisticated data augmentation strategies to enhance model robustness. Each architectural variant undergoes identical training protocols across 50 epochs with transfer learning from ImageNet pre-trained weights. Experimental results demonstrate that deeper ResNet configurations (ResNet101 and ResNet152) achieve superior taxonomic discrimination with classification accuracy exceeding 95.8%, while MobileNet_v2 establishes an optimal balance between accuracy (94.55%) and computational efficiency. Performance variations are critically analyzed with respect to architectural characteristics including residual connections, network depth, and parameter efficiency. This comprehensive architectural assessment provides valuable frameworks for selecting appropriate deep learning models across diverse ornithological applications, from high-performance research environments to resource-constrained field deployments.

### **1 Introduction**

Accurate identification of bird species constitutes a critical foundation for ecological research, conservation biology, and biodiversity assessment initiatives. The classification process supports essential scientific endeavors including population monitoring, habitat utilization studies, migration pattern analysis, and conservation status evaluation. Traditional identification methodologies relying on human expertise necessitate substantial specialized knowledge, demonstrate inherent subjectivity, and present significant

challenges when processing extensive image collections or analyzing specimens captured under suboptimal field conditions [1].

The taxonomic identification of avian species presents particular challenges due to substantial phenotypic variation within species (influenced by factors including age, gender, season, and geographical distribution) coupled with subtle distinguishing characteristics between closely related species. These complexities frequently result in identification uncertainties even among experienced ornithologists, particularly when limited morphological information is available from partial or obscured specimens [2].

The exponential advancement of computational capabilities and algorithmic frameworks over recent years has established transformative opportunities for applying artificial intelligence methodologies to biological classification challenges. In particular, convolutional neural networks have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in visual recognition tasks, significantly outperforming traditional computer vision approaches that relied on manually engineered feature extraction processes.

Hinton et al. introduced the dropout regularization technique that substantially enhanced neural network generalization capabilities by preventing feature co-adaptation during training processes [3]. This innovation effectively addressed critical overfitting challenges that previously limited deep learning applicability to complex classification scenarios with limited training data. Subsequently, He et al. developed groundbreaking residual learning frameworks that facilitated successful training of substantially deeper neural networks through identity mapping connections [4]. This architectural innovation enabled networks to scale to unprecedented depths while mitigating the vanishing gradient problem that had historically constrained deep network training.

Vaswani et al. proposed the transformer architecture incorporating sophisticated attention mechanisms for enhanced sequence modeling capabilities [5]. Though initially conceptualized for natural language processing applications, this architectural paradigm has subsequently inspired significant developments in visual recognition systems. Additionally, Brown et al. demonstrated critical scaling relationships in neural language models, establishing clear correlations between parametric complexity and performance characteristics across diverse computational tasks [6].

The present paper aims to conduct a systematic comparative analysis of three widelyimplemented CNN architectural families—specifically Residual Networks (ResNet), Visual Geometry Group (VGG), and MobileNet_v2—for avian species classification applications. The research utilizes a comprehensive image dataset spanning 525 distinct bird species and implements transfer learning methodologies to fine-tune pre-trained models for this specialized taxonomic classification task. The research objectives encompass performance evaluation across multiple dimensions including classification accuracy, computational efficiency, and generalization capabilities. Additionally, the paper seeks to provide practical insights regarding the relative advantages and limitations of each architectural approach, ultimately identifying optimal model selections for avian classification systems under varying operational constraints and deployment scenarios.

The significance of this research extends beyond theoretical architectural comparison, offering practical guidance for implementing automated bird species identification systems across diverse ecological applications including biodiversity monitoring programs, conservation initiatives, citizen science projects, and educational resources. Through comprehensive evaluation of the performance-efficiency trade-offs inherent in different CNN architectures, this investigation provides valuable frameworks for selecting appropriate deep learning models based on specific application requirements and deployment constraints.

# **2 Related Work**

### **2.1 Fine-Grained Visual Classification Approaches**

Fine-grained visual classification (FGVC) encompasses recognition tasks requiring discrimination between visually similar subcategories within broader taxonomic groups, such as identifying specific bird species, automobile models, or architectural styles. These classification scenarios present unique challenges characterized by substantial intra-class variation coupled with subtle inter-class differences, often requiring attention to discriminative features that may constitute only small portions of the overall image [7-10].

While general-purpose CNN architectures demonstrate exceptional performance in coarse-grained classification tasks, their effective application to FGVC frequently necessitates architectural modifications or supplementary techniques to enhance discrimination between visually similar categories. Recent research has explored various approaches to address these challenges, with particular emphasis on attention mechanisms, part-based modeling, and transfer learning methodologies.

Attention mechanisms have been successfully integrated into CNN frameworks to enhance focus on discriminative image regions, effectively ignoring irrelevant background information that might otherwise confound fine-grained classification. Li et al. proposed a multi-attention convolutional neural network specifically for bird species recognition, incorporating both channel and spatial attention modules to selectively emphasize informative features from critical anatomical regions including wing patterns, beak morphology, and plumage coloration [11]. This approach achieved significant performance improvements compared to standard CNN architectures on benchmark avian classification datasets.

Part-based modeling approaches decompose objects into constituent components, facilitating more effective representation of subtle discriminative features. Liu et al. implemented a part-based framework utilizing VGG as the backbone architecture, explicitly localizing key anatomical regions (head, wings, tail) prior to classification, achieving substantial accuracy improvements on the CUB-200-2011 dataset compared to holistic classification approaches [12]. This methodology demonstrates particular effectiveness for avian species recognition, where discriminative features frequently appear in specific anatomical regions.

Transfer learning methodologies have proven instrumental in fine-grained classification applications, particularly when confronted with limited training data availability. This approach leverages knowledge representations learned from large-scale general datasets (typically ImageNet) and adapts them to specialized classification tasks through fine-tuning processes. Kim et al. evaluated transfer learning effectiveness for avian classification, demonstrating that fine-tuned pre-trained architectures consistently outperformed models trained from random initialization, with performance differentials particularly pronounced in data-constrained scenarios [13].

#### **2.2 Avian Species Classification: Current Research Landscape**

Bird species classification represents an interdisciplinary research domain intersecting ecology, conservation biology, and computer science, with applications ranging from biodiversity monitoring to citizen science initiatives. Early computational approaches relied primarily on traditional computer vision methodologies incorporating manually engineered feature extractors such as color histograms, texture descriptors, or shape analysis techniques. However, these approaches demonstrated limited effectiveness when confronted with the

inherent complexity and variability of avian morphology across different species, ages, genders, and geographical distributions.

The transition to deep learning methodologies has substantially advanced avian classification capabilities, though comparative literature examining different architectural approaches remains relatively sparse. Smith et al. conducted a performance evaluation between traditional CNN architectures including AlexNet and GoogLeNet using a dataset comprising 500 bird species, determining that architectural depth significantly influenced classification accuracy, with deeper networks outperforming shallower configurations by approximately 15% [14]. However, this investigation predated modern architectural innovations including ResNet and MobileNet variants.

More recent research has explored hybrid methodological approaches combining CNN feature extraction with traditional machine learning classifiers. Rodriguez et al. implemented a dual-stage system utilizing ResNet-101 for feature extraction followed by support vector machine (SVM) classification, achieving 88% accuracy on the comprehensive NABirds dataset [15]. While demonstrating promising results, the computational requirements associated with deep ResNet configurations potentially limit practical deployment in resource-constrained scenarios.

Several critical gaps persist in the current research landscape. Firstly, systematic comparative evaluations between efficiency-oriented architectures (exemplified by MobileNet) and accuracy-prioritizing models (such as ResNet and VGG) specifically for avian classification tasks remain limited. Additionally, relatively few studies comprehensively address practical deployment considerations, including model compression strategies for edge computing devices, processing methodologies for lowquality field images, or real-time classification requirements for interactive applications.

### **2.3 Emerging Trends and Unresolved Challenges**

Particularly vision transformers (ViTs), have introduced novel paradigms for image classification tasks. These transformer-based approaches process images as sequences of patches, potentially capturing global contextual information more effectively than traditional CNN architectures. Thompson et al. applied vision transformers to bird species classification with promising experimental results (exceeding 91% accuracy on CUB-200- 2011), though their substantial computational requirements—exhibiting quadratic complexity relative to input dimensions—currently limit practical deployment in many application scenarios [16].

Data-efficient learning strategies represent another significant research direction, particularly relevant for ecological datasets that frequently exhibit size limitations, class imbalance characteristics, or taxonomic biases. Techniques including contrastive learning, self-supervised pre-training, and few-shot learning methodologies demonstrate considerable potential for enhancing classification performance in data-constrained scenarios. Wilson et al. implemented contrastive learning approaches to pre-train a ResNet architecture using unlabeled bird images, achieving significant accuracy improvements in experimental settings with limited labeled training data [17].

Despite encouraging progress, several fundamental challenges remain unresolved:

- 1. Domain Adaptation: Models trained on carefully curated datasets frequently demonstrate performance degradation when deployed in uncontrolled environments due to factors including background complexity, illumination variations, or image quality limitations. Developing robust domain adaptation techniques remains essential for practical field deployment of avian classification systems.
- 2. Interpretability: While deep learning models achieve impressive accuracy metrics, their decision-making processes frequently lack transparency, limiting their utility in

scientific applications requiring explanatory capabilities. Developing interpretable classification models that articulate their identification rationales represents an important research direction.

3. Ecological Representativeness: Most benchmark datasets exhibit geographical, taxonomic, and phenotypic biases that potentially limit generalization to global avian diversity. Expanding dataset coverage while maintaining annotation quality presents significant challenges requiring interdisciplinary collaboration between computer scientists and ornithologists.

This comprehensive literature review highlights the potential of convolutional neural networks for avian species classification while emphasizing the necessity for systematic architectural evaluations considering both performance characteristics and practical deployment constraints. Through comparative analysis of diverse architectural approaches, the current research aims to inform model selection decisions balancing accuracy requirements, computational efficiency considerations, and implementation feasibility for ecological applications.

# **3 Methodology**

### **3.1 CNN Architecture Selection and Configuration**

#### _3.1.1 ResNet Family_

The ResNet architecture family, characterized by its innovative residual learning framework, directly addresses the degradation problem encountered in training very deep neural networks. This approach implements skip connections (identity mappings) that enable gradient flow through the network during backpropagation, facilitating the training of substantially deeper networks than previously feasible.

The specific ResNet variants evaluated in this study include ResNet18, ResNet34, ResNet50, ResNet101, and ResNet152, with numerical designations indicating network depth (number of layers). Each architecture implements residual blocks where input features effectively bypass one or more convolutional layers, with outputs subsequently combined with the original input through element-wise addition. This mechanism preserves information flow throughout the network while enabling effective learning of complex feature representations.

#### _3.1.2 VGG Family_

The VGG architecture family represents a classic CNN design approach characterized by architectural simplicity and uniformity. This design philosophy employs small convolutional filters (3×3) arranged in increasingly deeper configurations.

The VGG variants included in this evaluation are VGG11, VGG13, VGG16, and VGG19, with numerical designations similarly indicating network depth. The VGG architecture follows a systematic pattern of convolutional layers with uniform filter sizes, followed by max-pooling operations for spatial dimensionality reduction, culminating in fully connected layers for classification. This architectural uniformity facilitates straightforward implementation and modification, though at the expense of parametric efficiency.

#### _3.1.3 MobileNet Family_

The MobileNet_v2 architecture specifically targets computational efficiency while maintaining competitive accuracy metrics, making it particularly suitable for deployment on resource-constrained platforms including mobile devices and embedded systems.

The foundational innovation in MobileNet_v2 involves depthwise separable convolutions, which decompose standard convolution operations into depthwise and pointwise components, substantially reducing computational requirements. The architecture additionally implements inverted residual blocks where the residual connections connect bottleneck layers rather than expanded representations, further optimizing memory utilization during inference.

All architectural variants undergo initialization with weights pre-trained on the ImageNet dataset (1.28 million images across 1,000 classes), subsequently fine-tuned specifically for the bird classification task using transfer learning methodologies. This approach leverages generalizable visual representations learned from diverse images while adapting network parameters to the specific characteristics of avian morphology.

### **3.2 Training Protocol and Optimization Approach**

Identical training protocols are implemented across all architectural variants. Each model undergoes training for precisely 50 epochs using consistent hyperparameter configurations. The optimization process employs the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 0.001, exponential decay rate parameters β₁=0.9 and β₂=0.999, batch size of 32, and categorical cross-entropy loss function appropriate for multi-class classification tasks.

The training process implements a comprehensive data augmentation strategy to enhance model generalization capabilities and mitigate overfitting risks. These augmentation techniques include:

- 1. Random horizontal flipping (50% probability): Introduces reflection invariance, particularly beneficial for bird species that exhibit bilateral symmetry.
- 2. Random rotation (±15 degrees): Enhances orientation invariance, addressing variations in bird posture and camera angle.
- 3. Random cropping with subsequent resizing: Promotes scale invariance and focuses attention on different anatomical regions.
- 4. Color jittering: Introduces controlled variations in brightness (±10%), contrast (±10%), and saturation (±10%), enhancing robustness to illumination variations.

These transformations significantly expand the effective training dataset while promoting invariance to common image variations encountered in field photography. Augmentation procedures are applied dynamically during training, with each image undergoing random transformation combinations for each epoch, effectively creating a virtually unlimited training dataset.

During the training process, model checkpoints are saved based on validation set performance, with early stopping mechanisms implemented to prevent overfitting while ensuring convergence. Learning rate scheduling dynamically adjusts the learning rate based on validation loss plateaus, implementing a reduction factor of 0.1 when validation performance fails to improve for 5 consecutive epochs.

To ensure reproducibility, all experiments utilize consistent random seeds for weight initialization, data partitioning, and augmentation processes. Training procedures are conducted using identical hardware configurations (NVIDIA Tesla V100 GPUs) to enable direct computational efficiency comparisons.

#### **3.3 Evaluation Metrics and Performance Assessment**

1. Accuracy: Represents the proportion of correctly classified samples relative to the total evaluation dataset size, calculated as:

$$Accuracy = (TP + TN)/(TP + TN + FP + FN)$$
(1)

While providing an intuitive overall performance indicator, accuracy alone proves insufficient for imbalanced class distributions.

2. Precision: Calculated as the ratio of true positive predictions to the total positive predictions, quantifying the model's ability to avoid false positive classifications:

$$Precision = TP/(TP + FP)$$
(2)

3. Recall (Sensitivity): Determined as the ratio of true positive predictions to the total actual positive samples, measuring the model's capacity to identify all instances of a particular class:

$$Recall = TP/(TP + FN)$$
(3)

- 4. F1-score: Computed as the harmonic mean of precision and recall, providing a balanced assessment incorporating both false positive and false negative error types:
- (4) 5) Confusion Matrix Components: Additional evaluation incorporates true positives (TP), true negatives (TN), false positives (FP), and false negatives (FN) metrics, enabling

detailed error analysis across the 525 bird species. This diverse metric collection provides comprehensive performance insights beyond simple accuracy measurements, facilitating nuanced architectural comparisons across multiple evaluation dimensions. Furthermore, computational efficiency metrics including parameter count, floating-point operations per second (FLOPS), and inference time are recorded to enable balanced assessment considering both performance and resource requirements.

# **4 Performance Comparison of CNN Architectures on the Bird Species Classification Task**

#### **4.1 Architectural Performance Analysis**

#### _4.1.1 ResNet Family Performance_

Experimental results demonstrate a consistent performance improvement correlating with network depth within the ResNet family, with ResNet152 achieving the highest overall accuracy (95.89%) among all evaluated architectures. This progressive improvement pattern (ResNet18: 93.56%, ResNet34: 93.41%, ResNet50: 94.32%, ResNet101: 95.77%, ResNet152: 95.89%) confirms that increased network depth facilitates the learning of more sophisticated feature hierarchies beneficial for distinguishing subtle morphological differences between similar bird species.

Notably, even the shallowest ResNet variant (ResNet18) achieves superior performance compared to any VGG configuration, despite having substantially fewer parameters. This observation emphasizes the architectural advantage conferred by residual connections independent of raw parameter count. The skip connections effectively mitigate the optimization difficulties typically encountered in training deep networks, enabling more effective backpropagation of gradients throughout the network.

The deeper ResNet variants (ResNet101 and ResNet152) demonstrate particularly impressive precision metrics (96.49% and 96.34% respectively), indicating exceptional capability in avoiding misclassifications across the 525 distinct bird species. These architectures excel specifically in distinguishing visually similar species that frequently cause identification challenges even for human experts.

#### _4.1.2 VGG Family Performance_

The VGG architectural variants consistently demonstrate lower performance across all evaluation metrics compared to both ResNet and MobileNet_v2 configurations. Interestingly, increasing network depth within the VGG family does not yield consistent performance improvements, with VGG16 slightly outperforming VGG19 in accuracy (91.89% vs. 91.58%) despite having fewer parameters.

This performance pattern suggests inherent architectural limitations in the VGG design for fine-grained classification tasks. The absence of skip connections potentially impedes effective gradient flow during backpropagation, particularly in deeper configurations. Additionally, VGG's fully connected layers contain the majority of model parameters yet contribute relatively little to discriminative feature learning for this specific task.

Error analysis reveals that VGG models particularly struggle with species exhibiting subtle morphological differences, frequently confusing visually similar species from the same family. This limitation suggests that VGG's linear architectural design may restrict its capacity to learn hierarchical feature representations necessary for fine-grained taxonomic discrimination.

#### _4.1.3 MobileNet_v2 Performance_

MobileNet_v2 achieves remarkable efficiency-performance balance, delivering the thirdhighest accuracy (94.55%) while utilizing approximately 70% fewer parameters than ResNet50 with comparable performance. This architectural efficiency derives primarily from depthwise separable convolutions and inverted residual structure, effectively preserving representational capacity while substantially reducing computational requirements.

The strong performance of MobileNet_v2 holds particular significance for field deployment scenarios, indicating potential for accurate real-time bird species identification on mobile devices or embedded systems with limited computational resources. Furthermore, MobileNet_v2 demonstrates excellent F1-score (94.36%), indicating balanced precisionrecall characteristics across the diverse species set.

#### **4.2 Practical Deployment Considerations**

Several practical considerations influence architectural selection for specific deployment scenarios:

1. Computational Efficiency: Parameter count serves as a proxy for computational requirements, with significant implications for deployment feasibility across different computing platforms. Table II presents the parameter count and relative computational efficiency for each evaluated architecture.

- 2. Inference Latency: Real-time applications including mobile field identification tools require minimal inference latency. Experimental measurements indicate that MobileNet_v2 achieves approximately 4× faster inference compared to ResNet101 on equivalent hardware, making it particularly suitable for interactive applications requiring immediate classification results.
- 3. Memory Requirements: Deployment on memory-constrained devices necessitates consideration of model size. VGG architectures prove particularly challenging in this regard due to their extensive fully connected layers, with VGG16 requiring approximately 39× more storage space than MobileNet_v2 despite delivering lower classification performance.

The practical implications of these efficiency considerations become particularly significant in ecological field research scenarios, where computational resources may be severely constrained. For these applications, MobileNet_v2 represents a compelling architectural choice, delivering near-top-tier accuracy with dramatically reduced computational requirements compared to both ResNet and VGG alternatives.

## **6 Conclusion**

In this comparative study, the paper have systematically evaluated the performance of prominent CNN architectures for bird species classification using a comprehensive dataset of 84,635 bird images spanning 525 species. The experimental results demonstrate that the deeper ResNet models (ResNet101 and ResNet152) achieve the highest classification performance. MobileNet_v2 provides a favorable trade-off between accuracy and computational efficiency, making it suitable for deployment in resource-limited scenarios. The VGG models, although historically significant, show relatively lower performance for this specific task.

Future research could explore additional lightweight CNN architectures, ensemble methods, and transfer learning strategies to further improve the accuracy of bird species classification. Additionally, investigating the application of these models in real-world scenarios and incorporating more advanced data augmentation techniques could also enhance the practicality and effectiveness of the classification systems.
