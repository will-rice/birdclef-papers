# **Passive acoustic monitoring in difcult terrains: the case of the Principe Scops‑Owl**

**Bárbara Freitas1,2,3,4,5,[6](http://orcid.org/0000-0001-6020-6236) · Yves Bas1,7 · Aloïs Robert1 · Claire Doutrelant1,8 [·](http://orcid.org/0000-0003-1893-3960) Martim Melo2,3,4,8,[9](http://orcid.org/0000-0003-1394-4361)**

Received: 1 November 2022 / Revised: 24 May 2023 / Accepted: 24 May 2023 © The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Springer Nature B.V. 2023

#### **Abstract**

Many species are difcult to study either due to their rarity, elusiveness, difcult access to their area of occurrence, or any combination of these. This can be particularly problematic for threatened species. Passive acoustic monitoring (PAM) is a recently developed survey technique that has shown great potential in addressing this problem for species that communicate through vocalizations. However, the large amount of data it generates can be diffcult to process manually. Here, we present an entirely automatic workfow to record and detect the vocalizations of a bird species that is both elusive (nocturnal) and restricted to difcult terrain in the most remote rainforests of an oceanic island: the recently discovered Principe Scops-Owl. Specifcally, we evaluated (i) the performance of the workfow to monitor the presence of the owl, (ii) we assessed the most suitable time for monitoring it; and (iii) we examined the potential of this species to present detectable vocal individual signatures. For 12 days, we deployed omnidirectional recording stations (AudioMoth devices) in 72 points along 10 transects that were surveyed during one night at the same time by observers in the feld. We trained TADARIDA, a machine learning software toolbox, to automatically detect owl calls. Results on the presence of the owl per site were similar for both methods. The automatic workfow showed that the owl is active during the whole night and the PAM recording setting should encompass at least the 21–23 h interval. Possibly, vocalizations had individual signatures—but the small sample size and temporal window prevented a defnite conclusion. The automatic workfow developed here is an efcient method to monitor the Principe Scops-Owl and can be easily adapted for other elusive vocal species.

**Keywords** Automated detection · Automatic recording units · Biodiversity surveys · Conservation · Machine learning · Passive acoustic monitoring

# **Introduction**

Efective biodiversity conservation depends on the availability of systematic data on species distribution, population trends, community composition and ecosystem structure (Collen et al. [2013](#page-20-0)). However, monitoring of rare or declining species can be problematic due to detectability or survey constraints—such as when these species are elusive and/or live in remote, difcult to access, locations. This problem can often be minimized, or even entirely circumvented, in the case of vocal species. For many vocal species, detectability of species-specifc vocalizations is much higher than visual detection—especially in closed habitats such as woodlands and forests (Mennill and Vehrencamp [2008](#page-21-0)) or any situation where there are strong visibility constraints (e.g., underwater environments, nocturnal species [Odom and Mennill [2010\]](#page-21-1)).

Acoustic communication is widespread in nature (e.g., insects, amphibians, birds, bats, and marine mammals) and can have multiple functions such as mate attraction, territory defense, rival intimidation, or social communication (Bradbury and Vehrencamp [2011](#page-19-0)). Vocalizations are species-specifc (and in many cases the most obvious diagnostic trait separating closely related species) making them suitable for species level surveys (Catchpole and Slater [2008\)](#page-20-1). Additionally, vocalizations can often encode individual signatures allowing to identify the same individual over many years as documented for many taxa including primates (Feng et al. [2014\)](#page-20-2), cetaceans (Mishima et al. [2015\)](#page-21-2), birds (Yee et al. [2016](#page-22-0)), and amphibians (Bee et al. [2001\)](#page-19-1). Such information can be used in estimating territory turnover and site fdelity (Galeotti and Sacchi [2001](#page-20-3); Delport et al. [2002](#page-20-4)) and in evaluating survival and movement of a population (Gilbert et al. [2002\)](#page-20-5), parameters that are of direct relevance for conservation (Terry et al. [2005](#page-21-3)).

Passive acoustic monitoring (PAM) is a non-invasive method for collecting acoustic signals of vocal animals by using automated recording units (ARUs) deployed in the feld (Browning et al. [2017](#page-19-2)). Recent years have seen both great technological improvements and a signifcant reduction of the costs of the ARUs, leading to a considerable growth in the use of PAM (Abrahams and Geary [2020](#page-19-3)). Today's ARUs, with a long battery life and programmable recording schedules, allow unattended recording (reducing observer bias) over long periods (Sueur et al. [2012\)](#page-21-4). They can be used either for multitaxa assessments or can focus on particular species by programming the frequency range to be recorded (Hill et al. [2019\)](#page-20-6). Furthermore, they allow for the systematic collection of large data volumes and the creation of standardized and permanent datasets, which can be repeatedly analyzed and validated by diferent researchers (Abrahams and Geary [2020](#page-19-3)).

For all these reasons, PAM now stands as a powerful tool for long-term and large-scale biodiversity monitoring, generating data that would otherwise require much larger research eforts to collect (Abrahams [2018\)](#page-19-4). PAM-based studies have even proved successful in the study of rare and endangered species, including those with low vocal activity inhabiting difcult to access locations, such as the African forest elephant, _Loxodonta cyclotis_, living in the Central Africa dense rainforests (Wrege et al. [2017\)](#page-22-1), or the Elfn woods warbler, _Setophaga angelae_, from the Puerto Rican Mountains (Campos‐Cerqueira and Aide [2016](#page-19-5)).

Nevertheless, studies using PAM generate a massive amount of data, require time-consuming analyses that often depend on specifc expertise, and create data storage and management constraints (Abrahams and Geary [2020](#page-19-3)), which all contribute to prevent a more widespread use of this method. Consequently, the current greatest challenge is to develop appropriate automatic recognition approaches that are able to produce reliable results even in complex acoustic environments. These post-processing methods have been steadily

![](_page_1_Picture_7.jpeg)

improving, as refected in the growing number of software and algorithms available (e.g., Aide et al. [2013;](#page-19-6) Bas et al. [2017](#page-19-7); Marsland et al. [2019;](#page-21-5) Roemer et al. [2021](#page-21-6); Kahl et al. [2021\)](#page-20-7). Here we took advantage of these emerging techniques to develop and provide a fully automated pipeline to survey a newly described bird species, the Principe Scops-Owl (_Otus bikegila_) from Príncipe Island, Gulf of Guinea (Melo et al. [2022](#page-21-7)).

Scops-owls make the genus _Otus_, a group of over 50 species of small owls widespread in Africa, Asia and Europe (König and Weick [2008](#page-20-8); Winkler et al. [2020](#page-22-2)). Many aspects of their biology remain to be investigated as their nocturnal habits and inconspicuous behavior make them hard to study (König and Weick [2008](#page-20-8); Winkler et al. [2020](#page-22-2)). One third of the _Otus_ species are within the threatened categories of the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) Red List (IUCN [2020](#page-20-9)) and all are protected under the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species Appendix II. They are more easily located through their simple and species-specifc vocalizations and, usually, it is this trait that leads to the discovery of new species, such as in the discovery of the Principe Scops-Owl (Melo and Dallimer [2009;](#page-21-8) Verbelen et al. [2016\)](#page-22-3). Field surveys following its discovery confrmed that this species is restricted to a small area in the native forests of Príncipe Island and, in particular, to the most remote ones of the south, and recommended for it to be classifed under the highest IUCN threat category, Critically Endangered (Freitas et al. [2023](#page-20-10)). Luckily, its entire area of occurrence is within the limits of a protected area, the Príncipe Obô Natural Park (Freitas et al. [2023](#page-20-10)). The Park authorities, in collaboration with local and international partners have been active in developing a long-term biodiversity monitoring strategy for the protected area. Although considered a priority, the efort required to monitor the Principe Scops-Owl is prohibitive in terms of human and funding resources. PAM, especially if coupled with automatic analysis of the data, holds great promise in overcoming this challenge.

The aim of this study was to develop and assess the feasibility of implementing a fully integrated automatic pipeline to monitor the Principe Scops-Owl. Specifcally, i) we evaluated the performance of PAM and automated call detection for mapping the presence of the species by comparing data obtained through an automatic workfow with data obtained by an observer-based survey; ii) we assessed the most suitable time for monitoring the owl; and iii) we conducted an initial analysis to examine whether the vocalizations of diferent individuals can be distinguished.

# **Methods**

#### **Study species and feld site**

The Principe Scops-Owl (Fig. [1C](#page-3-0)) is endemic from Príncipe Island. It was only described in 2022 (Melo et al. [2022\)](#page-21-7) after its discovery for science in 2016 (Verbelen et al. [2016](#page-22-3)), though suspicions on its presence go back to 1928 and surveys to fnd it took place from 2002 (Melo and Dallimer [2009](#page-21-8)). Príncipe Island (1°32'N–1°43ʹN, 7°20'E–7°28ʹE) is located in the Gulf of Guinea, 220 km west of the coast of central Africa (Fig. [1](#page-3-0)). Albeit with only c. 139 km2 , Príncipe is an important center of endemism (Jones [1994](#page-20-11)) and a global priority area for conservation (Stattersfeld et al. [1998;](#page-21-9) Buchanan et al. [2011](#page-19-8); Le Saout et al. [2013\)](#page-20-12). The whole of the island and its marine environment has been declared a UNESCO Biosphere Reserve (UNESCO [2012](#page-22-4)) and the Príncipe Obô Natural Park (PONP) covers c. 1/3 of its land area, including all remaining native forest (Law n. º 07/2006/Lei

## **Survey design**

After the discovery of the Principe Scops-Owl, an extensive survey of the island was carried out to determine its areas of occupancy and habitat preferences (see Freitas et al. [2023\)](#page-20-10). For that survey, sampling took place on points along transects, as recommended for difcult terrains (Buckland et al. [2001\)](#page-19-9). In this study, to develop and test the feasibility of a monitoring protocol integrating PAM with automatic song detection, we also used points along transects: a total of 80 sampling points were set along 10 transects (8.0±3.5 points per each transect; Fig. [1A](#page-3-0); second survey in Freitas et al. [2023\)](#page-20-10). All transects were within the PONP boundaries, in native forest or in its immediate vicinity. Eight of these were placed within the estimated area of distribution of the owl, whereas two transects were placed in its vicinity, in areas of native forest, with occupancy potential (Freitas et al. [2023\)](#page-20-10). Point counts within the transects were considered independent and used as sampling units as they were spaced 150 m apart in order to prevent double counting, similarly to what has been used for other owl species in similar environments (Borges et al. [2004](#page-19-10); Pilla et al. [2018](#page-21-10)). This distance also takes into account the inability of AudioMoths to capture owl calls at 96 m of distance (BF _pers. obs._). We considered these points as independent and we used them as sampling units. Their positions were registered using a Garmin GPS Map 62 s [Garmin, USA].

The study was conducted between 15 and 28 January 2019 (12 feld days). The month of January was selected because: i) it coincides with the breeding peak for most bird species in the oceanic islands of the Gulf of Guinea (Jones and Tye [2006](#page-20-13); Madeira [2018](#page-21-11)); ii) it is a period when the Principe Scops-Owl is vocally active (Melo and Dallimer [2009](#page-21-8); pers. obs.), likely actively defending breeding territories since the holotype caught in late May was undergoing a post-breeding moult (Melo et al. [2022\)](#page-21-7); and iii) it often coincides with a short dry spell within the rainy season, greatly improving the odds of conducting the survey.

#### **Observer‑based survey**

Each of the 10 transects was surveyed at a slow constant walking pace of approximately 1 km/h, during one night (no rain, no wind). Points were surveyed between 18:00 h and 21:43 h GMT. After arriving at each point, owl calls were registered during the frst two minutes: the direction and distance (roughly estimated from sound volume) of each call was annotated to subsequently determine if calls registered in neighboring points could be from the same individuals. When no owl was heard, we played a record (see online data repository for details) for one minute to stimulate responses using a speaker (Mini Amplifer/Speaker [RadioShack Technologies, USA]) and an iPod (iPod Touch 4th Generation [Apple In., USA]). Whenever an individual responded to playback, the record was stopped to prevent the bird from moving from its initial position. Additional calls heard along the transects but outside the sampling points were also registered and the GPS position of the observer noted. Whenever close enough, the vocalizations were recorded (sample rate of 44 kHz, gain level set to high) using an Edirol R-09HR [Roland, Japan] recorder and an MKE 400 [Sennheiser, Germany] microphone (frequency response: 50 Hz to 20 kHz). This survey corresponds to the 'second survey' reported in Freitas et al. [2023](#page-20-10).

## **Automatic recording survey**

The passive recording apparatus consisted in 10 AudioMoth automatic sound recording devices (Hill et al. [2018](#page-20-14)), hereafter referred to as AudioMoth. On the same day of the observer-based survey, we attached an AudioMoth to the nearest tree of each point count at approximately 1.70 m above the ground, for it to record for that night. The devices were placed within a plastic grip-sealed bag to protect from rain, and a refective tape was used for facilitating location of the points at night (Supplementary fle1 Fig. S2). We programmed AudioMoths to record from 16:40 h to 06:00 h GMT (earliest sunset at 17:40 h and earliest sunrise at 05:38 h), in cycles of 10 min followed by a 5 min pause. Considering that we had to use the same 10 AudioMoths without changing batteries or memory card, these cycles were chosen to conserve battery power and memory usage, and to facilitate data management by reducing the amount of recording time. AudioMoths were set to record at a sampling rate of 48 kHz, using default acoustic gain settings for the microphone preamplifer. Recordings were saved in.wav fle format, on SanDisk Extreme® microSDHC™ UHS-I—64 GB. By using these settings and recording schedule, storage was estimated to last 20 days and battery 23 days.

In the long transect with 17 sampling points (E, Fig. [1A](#page-3-0)), the 10 available AudioMoths were placed at every 300 m (rather than 150 m). Consequently, the number of points in which we deployed AudioMoths was diferent from the points of the observer-based survey. Specifcally, we deployed AudioMoths in 72 points of the 10 diferent transects (7.2±1.7 points per each transect) and in 3 extra points, totaling 75 points that were recording during one night in each point. The extra points, closer than 150 m of other points, were placed in areas where the presence of the owl was highly probable, including one point where an owl was accidentally fushed during the day, to increase the number of owls' recordings to be used in the signal recognizer parameterization. Data collection failed in 7 points due to recording errors of the device, resulting in a total of 34,535 min of recordings (about 575.6 h).

#### **Signal recognizer parameterization**

In order to detect automatically the recordings containing vocalizations of the Principe Scops-Owl, we used a workfow based on machine learning. For faster treatment of the recordings, each 10-min recording was split into 10 s fles (hereafter 10-s fles) using Kaleidoscope Pro v5.1.9 software (Wildlife Acoustics). We used TADARIDA v2.1 (a Toolbox for Animal Detection in Acoustic Recordings Integrating Discriminant Analysis; Bas et al. [2017\)](#page-19-7), that allows the detection (TADARIDA-D; <https://github.com/YvesBas/Tadarida-D>) and labelling (TADARIDA-L; <https://github.com/YvesBas/Tadarida-L>) of recorded sound events, and the classifcation (TADARIDA-C; <https://github.com/YvesBas/Tadarida-C>) of any acoustic data into known classes. Then we performed the following steps (Fig. [2](#page-6-0)):

1. **Detection of sound events**. We used TADARIDA-D (through TADARIDA-L treat function) to extract all detected sound events (DSEs) from the 10-s fles, which resulted in a total of 162,978 DSEs. DSEs are generated through TADARIDA by applying a hysteresis function (Bas et al. [2017\)](#page-19-7), which uses amplitude thresholds of 26 dB to detect and 20 dB to segment events in time and frequency, so that the least possible noise is included. TADARIDA-D sound detector also extracts a broad range of features (e.g. frequency, amplitude) on all DSEs.

- 2. **Building the initial reference sound database**. We used TADARIDA-L to manually label 12,177 DSEs into the following sound categories: "Owl" [referring to *Otus bikegila*; N =2510], "Parrot" [referring to the Grey Parrot *Psittacus erithacus*; N =4248], "Frog" [referring to the three species of frogs occurring in the forests of Príncipe; N =507], "Insect" [referring to all species of insects heard; N=3211] and "Noise" [referring to sound of rain, steps, human voice and mechanical noise; N =1701]. The 10-s fles containing those DSEs were selected mainly from points where we had listened to the owl, but included fles from all the points to account for sound diversity of localities (approximately 244 DSEs labelled per point). We created these labels by selecting all the DSEs of interest (in the graphical interface) within each 10-s fle and assigning them to one of the sound categories described above, by writing them in specifc flling felds. We also flled the "site" feld, which corresponded to each sampling point.
- 3. **Building and application of the classifer**. We used TADARIDA-C to build the classifer, a random forest automatic classifcation, using the reference sound database and applied this classifer to all the 10-s fles. For this, we used "Ta_Tc", "buildClassif",

![](_page_6_Picture_5.jpeg)

and "TadaridaC" R scripts provided in<https://github.com/YvesBas/Tadarida-C>and the "data.table" (Dowle and Srinivasan [2019](#page-20-15)) and "randomForest" packages (Liaw and Wiener [2002](#page-20-16)), in R (R Core Team [2017](#page-21-12)) using R Studio (RStudio Team [2015](#page-21-13)). In this step, TADARIDA-C aggregates and merges the label and features data to then build a series of 50 random forests of 10 classifcation trees each. During this process, the subsampling gradient setting makes the frst trees of the series use most of the available DSEs, whereas the last trees use an equal number of DSEs for most sound categories. The "site" feld is used for subsampling as well, making that each random forest uses 63% of available sites, which makes the training and testing data independent for each tree (see Bas et al. [2017](#page-19-7) for more details).

- 4. **Getting recognition scores**. Running the classifer returns a recognition score for each label, for each 10-s fle. The recognition score is a confdence index of the automatic identifcation (see Bas et al. [2017](#page-19-7) for more details) that ranges from 0 to 1, and indicates the probability of a label class being present in the sound fle.
- 5. **Checking errors**. In order to check the accuracy of the recognition scores for the owl calculated in the previous step, we divided them into 10 classes (0–0.1, 0.11–0.2, 0.21–0.3, 0.31–0.4, 0.41–0.5, 0.51–0.6, 0.61–0.7, 0.71–0.8, 0.81–0.9 and 0.91–1) and used a stratifed random sampling to listen to 43–46 10-s fles per class to check for the presence of the owl. We signaled the 10-s fles for which the recognition score did not correctly indicate presence or absence of the owl.
- 6. **Improving the detection**: using TADARIDA-L, we labelled fles with the most obvious mistakes, which consisted in fles where the owl is absent despite high recognition scores (between 0.9 and 1) and fles where the owl is present despite low recognition scores (between 0 and 0.3).

We repeated steps 3 to 6 until we were satisfed with the performance of the classifer (see below). This required 3 iterations corresponding to listening to 4.7 h of recordings, or about 0.8% of the whole dataset.

The fnal reference sound database included 17,692 labelled DSEs, of which 2730 were classifed as "Owl" (approximately 1.7% of the total number of DSEs), 5999 as "Parrot" (approximately 3.7% of the total number of DSEs), 595 as "Frog" (approximately 0.4% of the total number of DSEs), 6182 as "Insect" (approximately 3.8% of the total number of DSEs), and 2186 as "Noise" (approximately 1.3% of the total number of DSEs).

#### **Performance of the automatic recognizer**

To assess the performance of the automatic recognizer, we used the recognition scores given by TADARIDA classifer on four datasets: 1) all fles with the programmed time (16:40–06:00 h; full dataset); 2) fles from the beginning of the programmed time until two hours after sunset (16:40−19:44 h; sunset dataset); 3) fles from two hours before sunrise until the end of the programmed recording time (03:38–06:00 h; sunrise dataset); and 4) fles between those two periods (19:45–03:37 h; night dataset, referred also as night along the text). The three last datasets aimed at testing if avoiding analyzing dawn and dusk periods, when vocal activity of diurnal species is high (_pers. obs._), would result in the improvement of the performance of the automatic recognizer.

We calculated the area under the curve (AUC) with the "pROC" package (Robin et al. [2011\)](#page-21-14) for the four datasets. AUC represents how much the classifer is capable of distinguishing between the labelled classes, with the higher the AUC, the better the classifer.

![](_page_7_Picture_10.jpeg)

This is obtained by plotting the true positive rate (TPR: calculated as true positives/[true positives+false negatives]) against the false positive rate (FPR: calculated as false positives/[false positives+true negatives]).

We ran the subsequent analyses using the fles that obtained a recognition score for the owl higher than the threshold value, from the dataset with the highest AUC value. The threshold was obtained by minimizing the FPR to zero and selecting the highest possible TPR.

### **Inference of number of individuals from recordings**

The number of individuals per point was determined by randomly sampling the 10-s fles from the dataset for which the automatic recognizer performed better (see Results), in each of the sampling points of the 10 transects. Periods with playback and capturing events and the three points close to capture locations (used for Melo et al. [2022](#page-21-7); Fig. [1](#page-3-0)A) were deleted from the analysis. We used only recordings with a recognition score above the selected threshold (see Results). Fourteen sampling points had less than 10 fles above that threshold, so they were all analyzed. After this, the dataset consisted in 1082 fles from 34 sampling points.

To determine the number of individuals calling in each sampling point we manually inspected the spectrograms of the selected 10-s fles. On the basis of the characteristics of the Principe Scops-Owl calls (Melo et al. [2022](#page-21-7)), we used the following criteria to estimate the number of individuals:

- (1) when the internote interval (from the end of one note to the beginning of the next note) was 1.046±0.053 s (value for the internote interval of this species) and repetitive notes appear in the spectrogram, it corresponded to one individual (Supplementary fle1 Fig. S1a);
- (2) if the internote interval was longer than 1.046±0.053 s (which happened when, in the case of the main vocalization, an owl gives just one note—very rare—or in the case of the type two call – often), we used diferences in amplitude between the notes in the spectrogram to identify how many individuals were calling; overlapping notes allowed to identify two individuals (Supplementary fle1 Fig. S1b);
- (3) internote intervals shorter than 1.046±0.053 s allowed to identify more than one individual and we used diferences in amplitude between the notes to identify how many individuals were calling (Supplementary fle1 Fig. S1b and c). When noise was too high and recordings were from owls too far away to allow identify diferences, it was only possible to identify up to two individuals using the criteria of the internote duration, already described.

These criteria were made under the following assumptions: (i) Principe Scops-Owl is a territorial species and territories do not change over time. Territoriality is ubiquitous in _Otus_ species (König and Weick [2008](#page-20-8)) and was supported for this species by the fact that we captured the same Principe Scops-Owl individual in the same location on two diferent occasions (July 2018 and January 2019; Melo et al. [2022\)](#page-21-7); (ii) distances among recording points were greater than the diameter of home ranges (Freitas et al. [2023](#page-20-10)), so that individual owls were not double-counted at adjacent stations; (iii) home ranges do not overlap, as shown for other scops-owl species (Najmi-Hanis et al. [2016\)](#page-21-15); and (iv) birds did not move during the recording period of each fle (10 s), which is consistent with feld observations

![](_page_8_Picture_10.jpeg)

of the owl singing continuously from trees during several minutes (BF, pers. obs.). Thus, we considered that it is possible to record the same individual in the same territory on multiple visits.

The highest number of individuals detected on each spectrogram of the recordings from each point was compared with the data obtained from the observer-based survey for that same point.

### **Most suitable time to automatically monitor the Principe Scops‑Owl**

In order to determine the most suitable time to automatically monitor the Principe Scops-Owl, we assessed the periods in which this species is vocally active within the hours of the dataset for which the automatic recognizer performed better (see Results). For this, the number of 10-s fles where an owl was automatically detected through our workfow was counted for each of the following time intervals: 19:45–20:00, 20:00–21:00, 21:00–22:00, 22:00–23:00, 23:00–00:00, 00:00–01:00, 01:00–02:00, 02:00–03:00, 03:00–03:37, for each point count. The three extra points that distanced less than 150 m from other points were deleted from the analysis to avoid double counting of individuals. The three points close to capture locations (Fig. [1](#page-3-0)A) were also deleted to avoid vocal activity bias due to the use of playback. Because the automatic workfow does not provide number of notes detected for each fle, vocal activity was assessed by using the proportion of 10-s fles with detections per time interval. Mean of proportion of detection was calculated for each time interval estimated from 34 sampling points (1 night in each) distributed in 8 transects and this was plotted using the R package "ggplot2" (Wickham [2016](#page-22-6)). These 34 points were the ones for which the automatic workfow was able to detect 10-s fles with the owl's call. To identify the hours with the highest calling activity for the owl, we ftted a generalized linear model (GLM, quasibinomial) with the proportion of detection in each time interval as the response variable and time interval as a predictor (nine levels), using the R package "stats" (R Core Team [2017\)](#page-21-12). Tukey's post hoc test was performed to test for diferences between time intervals, using the R package "multcomp" (Hothorn et al. [2008\)](#page-20-17).

As a complementary analysis, and aiming at assessing spatial autocorrelation, we plotted a variogram using the "nlme" (Pinheiro and Bates [2023\)](#page-21-16) R package. This uses the distance between points to assess the correlation of samples.

# **Vocal signatures of individuals**

We explored whether the vocalizations of diferent individuals could be distinguished by using AudioMoth recordings. We selected only the fles of the frst half of the distribution of the values of note amplitude (parameter Ampm50 given by TADARIDA-C) for analysis. This variable represents the median amplitude of call groups (within each 10-s fle), and TADARIDA amplitude measures are intrinsic measures of signal-to-noise ratio, thus it should be a reliable indicator of the good quality of the recordings (Bas et al. [2017](#page-19-7)). This parameter was used previously for measuring classifcation performance according to call quality and has proven to be useful (see Roemer et al. [2021](#page-21-6)).

Song recordings were sampled using a 16-bit accuracy and a sampling rate converted to 12,000 Hz in Avisoft-SASLab pro version 4.3 (Avisoft Bioacoustics, Berlin). We selected a minimum of 3 and up to 22 notes from each individual owl (8.0±4.4 notes), identifed following the criteria mentioned above, and extracted 15 acoustic variables following the methods of previous studies (Melo et al. [2022\)](#page-21-7): F1, frequency at start; F2, frequency at

![](_page_9_Picture_9.jpeg)

end; F3, frequency at 25% of total duration; F4, frequency at midpoint; F5, frequency at 75% of total duration; F6, frequency at maximum amplitude; F7, maximum frequency; F8, minimum frequency; DT1, total duration; DT2, time to maximum amplitude; DT3, time to maximum frequency; DF1, frequency drop from start to end (F2-F1); DF2, frequency range (F7-F8); DFT1, slope from 25 to 75% of total duration ([F5-F3]/∆t); DFT2, slope from midpoint to end ([F2-F4]/∆t). We analyzed spectrograms using a Fast Fourier Transformation size of 512 points, a 100% frame size and a temporal resolution overlap of 87.5% (fat top window type), resulting in a frequency resolution of 86 Hz and a temporal resolution of 4.5 ms. Frequencies were band-pass fltered between 0.65 kHz (high-pass) and 1.6 kHz (low-pass) using a frequency domain transformation to focus on the vocal signal. The eraser option (that allows to delete the noise manually) was used just as a last resort when better recordings were not available.

We had a total of 191 notes from 24 individuals (not all individuals detected in our recordings could be used due to overlapping notes, notes that were too faded out or cut, probably due to distance and vegetation efects). Histograms showed that data was normally distributed for all the acoustic variables, except for DT3. For this variable we performed a log transformation calculated as log(x\*100+1).In order to assess if the vocalizations of the Principe Scops-Owl allow to determine the identity of individuals, we tested whether the variation among individual owls was greater than the variation within individual owls by calculating the proportion for individuality coding (PIC). This was calculated by comparing the within to between individual coefcients of variation (CV) of the measured characteristics. For each variable, we calculated CVw (within individual CV) corrected for small samples sizes (Scherrer [1984\)](#page-21-17) according to the formula CV=100\*(SD/mean) × (1+¼N), and CVb (between individual CV) according to the formula CV=100 × (SD/mean), where SD is the standard deviation of the acoustic variables (see below) and N is the number of calls per owl. We calculated mean CVw by averaging CVw of the total number of owls. PIC values were calculated as PIC=mean CVw/CVb. A PIC value higher than 1 for a given variable indicates that variability between individuals is higher than within individuals suggesting that this variable may potentially carry individual information (Lengagne [2001;](#page-20-18) Odom et al. [2013\)](#page-21-18). PIC values higher than two are considered high, and thus likely to carry individual information (Robisson et al. [1993](#page-21-19); Lengagne et al. [1997\)](#page-20-19).

To test whether calls could be correctly assigned to the predetermined individual owls, we followed D'Amelio et al. [\(2017](#page-20-20)) by conducting a Principal Component Analysis (PCA) with size-standardized variables, using the R packages "FactoMineR" (Lê et al. [2008](#page-20-21)) and "factoextra" (Kassambara and Mundt [2017](#page-20-22)), in order to avoid correlation of variables. Subsequently, we used the frst three principal components as explanatory factors in a linear discriminant analysis (R function "lda" (Ripley et al. [2020](#page-21-20)) from the "MASS" package (Venables et al. [2002](#page-22-7)) with the individual code as a predictor. We used the parameters obtained to predict the proportion of cases in which the notes were assigned to the correct individual (R function "predict": Ripley et al. [2020\)](#page-21-20).

In order to access vocal stability in this species (i.e., whether the notes of the same individual maintain the same characteristics over time), we used recordings taken six months apart from the same female owl (confrmed by the ring code) that was manually recorded previously to being captured in 2018 and 2019 (see Melo et al. [2022](#page-21-7) for more details). We selected two high-quality recordings from each year with 46 s to 1 min 13 s of duration each. They have been recorded with the same equipment and settings as this study, from a distance of up to 30 m of the owl and with minimal background noise, to minimize measurement errors, and included spontaneous calls and calls induced by playback. Spectrograms were analyzed with the same settings described previously and the same 15

![](_page_10_Picture_5.jpeg)

variables were extracted. In total, we measured 57 notes for 2018 (from the 58 that were available) and 26 for 2019 (from the 37 that were available) and we performed T-tests to assess if acoustic variables were signifcantly diferent between years.

## **Performance of the automatic recognizer**

The night dataset (records from 19:45 to 03:37 h) had the highest AUC value (AUC=0.977; Table [1\)](#page-11-0), indicating that the classifer is more efcient in this dataset. The next best AUC values were for the sunset dataset, which includes the dusk chorus (records between 16:40 and 19:44 h; AUC=0.949) and for the full dataset (AUC=0.948). The sunrise dataset, coinciding with the dawn chorus (records between 03:38 and 06:00 h), fared the worst (AUC=0.882).

Selecting the night dataset, minimizing the false positive rate (FPR) to zero, and selecting the highest possible true positive rate (TPR; 0.839), the threshold obtained for the recognition score was 0.655. This also indicates that the classifer is more efcient in this dataset, compared to the others, in the way that we can trust a lower recognition score to select recordings with the owl calls.

## **Vocal signatures of individuals**

The PICs (proportions for individuality coding) of all 15 variables had a value higher than one, indicating that all vocal parameters were more consistent within individuals, i.e., variation was greater between than within individuals (Table [2\)](#page-14-0). Therefore, all the variables were individually distinct, being potentially useful for individual identifcation as tested below.

In the PCA analysis including the 15 acoustic variables, the frst three PCs represented 70.6% of the variation (Supplementary fle2 Table S2). PC1 (37.0% of the variance) mostly represented frequency variables of the note (negatively correlated with F1-8) but also DT2 (positively correlated). PC2 (21.1% of the variance) mostly represented temporal characteristics of the note, being positively correlated with DT1, DT3, DF1 and DF2. PC3 (12.5% of the variance) was positively correlated with DFT1 and DFT2. The plot of PC1 and PC2 showed an overall overlap of the group of notes of each individual (Supplementary fle1 Fig. S4). The DFA, using PC1, PC2 and PC3, correctly assigned 50.8% of the notes to 24 individuals, for which we had between 3 and 22 notes (Supplementary fle2 Table S3). When analyzing only the 5 individuals for which a minimum of 10 notes was available, the plot of PC1 and PC2 demonstrated the clusters of the fve individuals with almost no overlap (Supplementary fle1 Fig. S5) and the DFA score improved considerably, correctly assigning 79.7% of the notes (Supplementary fle2 Table S4).

All the variables measured for the vocalizations of the same individual female recorded in 2018 and 2019 did not difer signifcantly (T-test P>0.05), except for DT1, DT2, DT3 and DFT1 (Supplementary fle2 Table S5), suggesting that the vocal parameters of this owl species have a low within individual variability between years.

# **Discussion**

We demonstrated the suitability of Passive Acoustic Monitoring (PAM), coupled with an automatic data analysis pipeline, to survey the recently discovered Principe Scops-Owl. The method developed here performed well, providing almost identical data on the distribution of the Principe Scops-Owl and the number of individuals per sampling point as the much more costly and difcult observer-based survey. It also allowed to assess the most suitable periods to monitor this species, something never done before and particularly strenuous if using observers on the ground. Of additional interest were the preliminary results suggesting that it is possible to identify diferent individuals based on their unique vocal signatures when many notes are analyzed. If confrmed with additional data and included in the automatic workfow, this would increase greatly the detail of the population monitoring eforts, improving the quality of management decisions, by providing more precise population estimates. Overall, and independently of its potential for identifying distinct individuals, the automatic workfow developed here, from data collection in the feld to detection of vocalizations in the recordings, can be applied straight away to monitor, at relatively low cost and efort, this species that likely qualifes to the Critically Endangered category of the IUCN Red List (Freitas et al. [2023](#page-20-10)). This method has the potential to be

![](_page_14_Picture_6.jpeg)

adapted to a wide array of species and is particularly relevant for species difcult to train and include in more generalist machine learning methods.

#### **An automatic workfow to monitor the Principe Scops‑Owl**

We developed an automatic workfow to survey the rare and elusive Principe Scops-Owl that greatly reduces the human resources, time, and associated costs required for observerbased surveys. As such, this method will allow to greatly extend the coverage area and the frequency of the surveys, besides removing potential bias linked to diferences in the experience of the observers carrying out direct surveys. Thus, this workfow should be incorporated into the biodiversity monitoring strategies of the PONP.

The integrated workfow combines the deployment in the feld of automatic recording units (AudioMoth devices) with an automatic data analysis pipeline (implemented with the TADARIDA software). We have shown that our method performs as well as observerbased surveys for detecting the presence of owls. This is a fundamental parameter to monitor populations by allowing to track changes in distribution with time. Recent studies have likewise shown a comparable or better performance of PAM relatively to human observation (e.g. Darras et al. [2019,](#page-20-23) and see Abrahams [2018](#page-19-4)).

The low cost, small size and low power requirements of AudioMoth devices (Hill et al. [2018,](#page-20-14) [2019\)](#page-20-6) makes them suitable for large scale surveys. This is of particular signifcance for gaining a better understanding of the occupancy area of the Principe Scops-Owl within the native forests where it occurs (Freitas et al. [2023\)](#page-20-10). The 2007 surveys to map what was then named the 'mystery call' (Melo and Dallimer [2009\)](#page-21-8), and the observer based-surveys of 2018 and 2019 (Freitas et al. [2023\)](#page-20-10) were mostly unable to reach the mid and high elevation areas except for a small area around 600 m a.s.l., as progression at night in such terrain is impossible. The deployment of AudioMoths in these areas during daytime is also challenging but possible and will allow to get a defnite estimate of the efective range and habitat preferences of the species. At present, data suggest that the owl prefers the lowland rainforest (below 300 m a.s.l.), which would result in a very narrow occupancy area—but this may partially refect the sampling bias. We recommend therefore to frst use this method to survey the areas not visited so far, in order to gain a complete understanding of the distribution of the species. Based on this, a network of points within transects covering representative areas of the area of occupancy of the owl can be designed to monitor the species in the long-term. AudioMoths can also be deployed, episodically, in places of high habitat suitability but where no records have ever been obtained—with the purpose of confrming its absence or registering new occurrences.

An efcient management of power and data storage use is key to minimize the number of visits to the feld. For the Principe Scops-Owl, we suggest setting recording to a period encompassing at least the 21–23 h interval, corresponding to the time-window with the highest vocal activity within the period for which the automatic workfow had the highest accuracy. Using only this interval and the same recording cycles and settings as in this study, storage would last 138 days and battery 130 days. Dusk and dawn periods are to be avoided as they coincide with high vocal activity of many of the diurnal species (Torben and Mathevon [2002](#page-21-21); Robert et al. [2019\)](#page-21-22), making the method less efective in detecting the owl and leading to false positives triggered by similar vocalizations of species such as those of the Grey Parrot. A sample rate of 48 kHz and a schedule of recording for 10 min followed by 5 min in pause was suitable to properly survey this species, but a sample rate

![](_page_15_Picture_7.jpeg)

of 24 kHz could be used to save memory and we also encourage more tests regarding the recording cycle and schedule to further saving battery power and card memory.

The automatic workfow coupled with manual inspection of the spectrograms further provided estimates of the number of owls at each sampling point. Having this information would allow tracking changes in the relative abundance with time, being also a fundamental parameter to monitor populations. However, these estimates should still be used with caution since the exact home ranges for this species are unknown (we used approximations from other related species) and, although unlikely from our results, it might happen that the same individual few between points. We advise future research to further investigate this and to ensure that the same individual is not double-counted in adjacent points. Further eforts towards developing and improving the ability of the method to identify individuals based on their unique vocal signatures (see below) will be immensely valuable for this purpose.

## **Advantages of the automatic workfow method**

#### **Vocal activity patterns**

The use of PAM coupled with automatic data analysis allowed to describe the vocal activity pattern of the Principe Scops-Owl throughout the night period. Obtaining the same information from observers would require a much larger efort and/or would result in much smaller sample sizes. Vocal activity of territorial species such as scops-owls can constitute an informative proxy on breeding periods (Catchpole and Slater [2008\)](#page-20-1). In equatorial climates, such as on Príncipe Island, seasonality is reduced and reproduction may occur throughout the year (Stutchbury and Morton [2001\)](#page-21-23). Still, breeding peaks do tend to occur, as documented for other species of the Gulf of Guinea islands (Jones and Tye [2006;](#page-20-13) Madeira [2018\)](#page-21-11), but can be difcult to determine without long-term feldwork eforts. To monitor the vocal activity of the Principe Scops-Owl throughout the year, it will be needed to choose a few transects in areas of high owl density and easy access. A three-year study will likely provide sufcient information to infer the breeding activity pattern—an information particularly relevant for conservation eforts.

This automatic workfow can be applied straight away to monitor the Principe Scops-Owl, but if a more specifc algorithm is required and more recordings are available, this method can be refned in order to be accurate when using full-day datasets.

#### **Individual identifcation**

We showed that all 15 spectral and temporal variables measured can most probably be used in individual identifcation, with fve variables having particularly high PIC (proportions for individuality coding) values. These included frequency at start and end, which were also found to be particularly important in the vocal signatures of the Sunda Scops-Owl [_Otus lempiji_; (Yee et al. [2016\)](#page-22-0)]. Still, as the main call of the owl consists in a single note, diferences between individuals are expected to be subtle. In our sample size of 24 individuals, and in spite of high PIC values for the individual variables, only 51.3% of the notes were correctly assigned to the pre-defned individuals. Increasing the number of reference notes per individuals may increase the performance of this method as for the fve individuals for which we had a minimum of 10 notes available, the percentage of notes correctly assigned increased to 79.7%. This is closer to levels found in other species of the family,

![](_page_16_Picture_9.jpeg)

such as in the European Scops-Owl [_Otus scops_; 92.1%, 9 individuals; (Galeotti and Sacchi [2001](#page-20-3))], the Ural Owl [_Strix uralensis_; 84.5%, 15 individuals or 86.5%, 16 individuals; (Zhou et al. [2020](#page-22-8))], or the Tawny Owl [_Strix aluco_; 96.9%, fve individuals; (Choi et al. [2019\)](#page-20-24)]. We have also collected anecdotal evidence suggesting that the vocal signatures are likely to be stable in this species, as demonstrated in other species of the genus (Dragonetti [2007;](#page-20-25) Yee et al. [2016](#page-22-0)): out of the 15 acoustic variables of an individual of known identity (captured and ringed, recaptured) recorded six months apart, only 4 were signifcantly different between the two periods.

If stable vocal signatures are confrmed to be present in the Principe Scops-Owl this will increase greatly the value of the automatic monitoring protocol developed here (Terry et al. [2005\)](#page-21-3). We encourage future research to test and include the automatic identifcation of diferent individuals in this workfow. Even if the automatic workfow does not perform well, manual inspection of spectrograms can be used at the fnal stage of the process (as we did for this study). Multiple studies have shown how vocal individuality can be a powerful parameter for monitoring populations across years, providing new insights on site fdelity, territory turnover, habitat quality, and even permitting the estimation of population demographics such as reproductive success or mortality rates (Delport et al. [2002](#page-20-4); Tripp and Otter [2006;](#page-22-9) Nagy and Rockwell [2012;](#page-21-24) Yee et al. [2016\)](#page-22-0).

Since frequency diferences between males and females are described for other species of this genus, we motivate future studies to investigate if this applies to this species, and, if so, to include it in the automatic workfow developed here. The ability to automatically identify the sex would further improve the value of the surveys, allowing to estimate the sex ratio of the population, a central parameter in population dynamics (Ancona et al. [2017\)](#page-19-11).

# **Applicability of the method for other species**

The integrated automatic workfow we developed to survey the Principe Scops-Owl based on its vocalizations can be adapted to a wide array of species. The performance of the method will depend mainly on the capacity of TADARIDA to correctly assign sounds to the species of interest. This will depend on the vocalization in itself (how diagnostic, distinct, it is) and on the local soundscape (ambient noise, degree of overlap with other vocalizing species, and context [see Metcalf et al. [2022\]](#page-21-25)). These variables will determine frstly the feasibility of using this method for a given species. For species found to be potentially suitable to be surveyed by this method, these variables will in turn determine the initial time investment required to develop an accurate automatic detection algorithm. In any case, the initial time-consuming process will save much more time in downstream analysis steps. More signifcantly, this is a once-of efort, as the algorithm produced for a given species in a given area can then be used forever. Evidently, quick surveys of a given area, such as rapid biodiversity assessments, may not beneft as much from this method, unless algorithms for species of interest are already available (such as for bats: Roemer et al. [2021](#page-21-6) or birds: Kahl et al. [2021](#page-20-7)). Additional work in the feld, with observers and/or a pilot PAM study, will be useful to describe the vocal activity patterns of the species of interest in order to choose the recording times that maximize data collection and minimize battery and memory use.

The simple, distinct, loud, and frequent vocalizations of the Principe Scops-Owl have facilitated the development of an automatic recognition method for the species. The fact that this species is the only nocturnal bird of its community has also contributed to the high

![](_page_17_Picture_7.jpeg)

performance of our method. It is expected that the method developed here will be easy to adapt to species with vocalizations with similar characteristics and/or living in communities with simple soundscapes (concerning levels of ambient noise and inter-specifc vocal interference). In Príncipe, this method can be adapted for monitoring the three endemic frog species; all vocal distinct species inhabiting the same areas as the owl and with expected similar activity periods.

In general, this automatic workfow should be particularly suited for most vocal organisms of oceanic islands communities. These species-poor communities are often assemblages of highly distinct species derived from the vagaries of the colonization process (Thornton [2007\)](#page-21-26). In birds, for example, archipelago radiations that lead to the co-occurrence of closely related species are a very rare event and, instead, most species derive from independent colonization of unrelated lineages (Valente et al. [2020\)](#page-22-10). As a result, on oceanic islands: i) the depauperate faunas create environments with lower ambient noise and reduced species vocal interference, relatively to the similar habitats on the mainland (Robert et al. [2019\)](#page-21-22), and ii) the idiosyncratic species assemblages reduces the probability of the co-occurrence of similar vocalizations, facilitating species identifcation. As oceanic islands are both hotspots of endemism and of human-driven extinctions (Whittaker et al. [2017\)](#page-22-11), the method developed here can be particularly useful to expand long-term monitoring of vocal species of conservation concern. Even more so, because local endemic species usually are not included in more general machine learning algorithms such as BirdNET (Kahl et al. [2021\)](#page-20-7).

# **Conclusion**

The combined use of automatic recorders and automated vocalizations detection algorithms showed to be a powerful tool for surveying the Principe Scops-Owl—a highly threatened nocturnal species from the forests of Príncipe Island, only confrmed to exist in 2016 (Verbelen et al. [2016](#page-22-3)). The integrated automatic survey method developed here can play a key role in the species' conservation action plan, by providing detailed information on population fuctuations, in time and space, at low-cost and with reduced human efort. It can also provide further insights into the vocal night activity and behavioral ecology of the owl. The automatic workfow method followed by manual inspection of spectrograms has room to be improved in what regards its ability to identify distinct individuals that, together with the potential to identify the sex of vocalizing birds, would provide valuable information on population dynamics of this little-known species. The automatic pipeline can also be adapted to other vocal taxa. We greatly encourage to take advantage of the rapid proliferation, low-cost, less demanding, and time-consuming analyses of these methods to assess biodiversity over a range of spatial and temporal scales. This methodology has the potential to become a widespread monitoring tool for long-term surveys. It will certainly prove to be of great value for the conservation of elusive species inhabiting areas of difcult access.
