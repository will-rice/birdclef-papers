## Abstract

Bird species identification is a prerequisite to biodiversity tracking, ecological studies, and wildlife conservation. Clearly identifying the species of birds enables researchers to monitor populations, detect changes in habitats, and inform effective conservation measures. However, the classification carried out traditionally by bird experts is usually time-consuming, laborious, and subject to human error, necessitating the use of strong automated mechanisms. In this research, we present a framework based on the use of deep learning techniques to classify bird species based on convolutional neural networks (CNN). VGG19 and EfficientNet-B3 were used, implemented, and analyzed on a dataset comprising 200 bird species with diverse visual features, totaling 2,400 images. Detailed experiments were conducted to determine the output of the competing models based on accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. VGG19 achieved an accuracy of approximately 79.70 percent, while EfficientNet-B3 delivered superior performance with 98 percent accuracy, demonstrating the benefit of optimized compound scaling and efficient feature extraction. Our results indicate that birds can be automatically recognized very effectively based on the use of deep CNN-based models, representing important instruments used in ecological research as well as biodiversity conservation. This framework is capable of being used to support the conduct of largescale monitoring programs as part of developing smart wildlife management systems. The significant performance gap between the two architectures highlights the importance of model selection in fine-grained classification tasks. Both VGG19 and EfficientNetB3 were fine-tuned using ImageNet pre-trained weights, so as to leverage the transfer learning for better performance on the bird dataset. Such initialization helped the models to retain rich low-level visual features learned from large-scale natural images and accelerated the convergence on our limited dataset. By transferring these generalized representations, the networks required less training samples and resulted in significantly higher accuracy on fine-grained bird species recognition.
