# Using non-negative matrix factorisation to facilitate efficient bird species richness surveys

### **Abstract**

- This paper considers the task of learning spectra of bird vocalisations to facilitate determination of unique
- species from environmental recordings. Although a variety of automated methods have been successfully
- applied for acoustic recognition of specific bird species, they normally require a well labelled training dataset.
- However, such methods are less effective in case of non-targeted multi-species inventories.
- In this study, we propose a non-negative matrix factorisation based clustering method to address this problem.
- First, the non-negative matrix factorisation is applied to decompose spectrogram matrices into temporal
- coefficient matrices and spectral profile matrices. By clustering temporal coefficients of a spectrogram matrix,
- we average the corresponding spectral profiles that fall into the same clusters as distinct bird vocalisations.
- Under the assumption that no species share similar vocalisations and any species has a low diversity of
- vocalisations, we utilise the spectral profiles to indicate distinct bird species. A greedy algorithm is then
- designed to sample audio clips so that each audio clip maximise the number of new spectral profiles. One is
- enabled to enhance the efficiency of determining the bird species richness from environmental recordings. The
- efficiency is validated with bird annotations provided by experienced ornithologists.
- **Key words**: non-negative matrix factorisation; clustering; acoustic sampling; bird species richness

# **1 Introduction**

- The deployment of acoustic sensors provides a continuous and less invasive approach to record environmental
- sounds at large spatiotemporal scales. The availability of acoustic data can be used to monitor vocal species
- such as insects and frogs (Brandes et al., 2006), birds (Acevedo et al., 2009), and bats (Russo and Voigt,
- 2016). Among these species, birds have been widely recognised as good indicators of biodiversity because
- they can rapidly reflect environmental changes, they spread over a large landscape, and their ethology is well
- understood.
- A ubiquitous characteristic of bird vocalisations is their diversity. On one hand, inter-specific vocalisations
- diverge in time and frequency (Michat and Osiejuk, 2010); on the other hand, intra-specific vocalisations vary
- depending on locations, temperature, or vegetation of a particular landscape (Kosicki and Chylarecki, 2012).
- A low signal-to-noise ratio of environmental recordings also makes automated detection difficult. In this
- context, the signal refers to bird vocalisation that is of interest while the noise refers to any unwanted sound
- such as geophony (rain or wind) and anthropophony (mechanical sounds). Additionally, simultaneous bird
- vocalisations pose another challenging problem (Briggs et al., 2012).
- Despite the acoustic complexity of environmental recordings, human beings are able to differentiate a variety
- of bird vocalisations by listening to recordings and visually inspecting the spectrograms. Manual analysis can
- quickly become intractable due to the escalating volume of recordings. An efficient alternative is the use of
- automated techniques to analyse the recordings. These techniques start with creating statistical models based
- on the mappings between instances and pre-defined labels. Once the models are created, incoming unlabelled
- instances can be automatically associated with the pre-defined labels.
- Bird species richness is one of the most important studies for biodiversity assessment (Kosicki and Chylarecki,
- 2014). It is a study of the number of unique bird species in a specific habitat within a specific period of time.
- We aim with this paper to develop an automated technique to enhance the efficiency of bird species richness
- surveys with environmental recordings. The problem is formulated as this: given a one-day recording with
- non-targeted multiple species inventories, identify the maximum number of unique bird species while

- listening to the minimum number of one-minute audio clips. We specifically focus on audio clips of one day
- because bird species compositions are relatively stable within this time frame in a specific habitat.

#### **1.1 Automated bird classification**

- Classification of bird species by their vocalisations lends itself to rapid analysis of environmental recordings.
- A typical classification system consists of two primary processes: feature extraction and classification
- (Brandes, 2008). Feature extraction is one of, if not the most, crucial steps in such a system. It is a process that
- utilises a value or a vector to represent bird vocalisations in the recording. A prevalent form for feature
- extraction is the spectrogram, which is a result of applying the short-time Fourier transform to a waveform
- signal. Features derived from the spectrogram can be exemplified by linear predictive coding and Mel-
- frequency cepstral coefficients (Kogan and Margoliash, 1998), sinusoidal pulses with time-varying amplitude
- and frequency (Harma, 2003), spectral peak tracks (Chen and Maher, 2006), and syllable pair histograms
- (Somervuo and Harma, 2004). They can be used individually or in combination to represent bird vocalisations.
- The classification process utilises statistical criteria to determine the mappings between the extracted features
- and the pre-defined labels. Various statistical criteria have been studied. Multivariate analysis (Martindale,
- 1980. and cross correlation (Clark et al., 1987) have been used for matching similar bird vocalisation. Other
- statistical criteria include artificial neural network (McIlraith and Card, 1997), hidden Markov models (Kogan
- and Margoliash, 1998), decision tree (Vilches et al., 2006), and support vector machine (Fagerlund, 2007).
- Recently, a multi-instance multi-label classification method has been proposed to tackle the problem of
- simultaneous vocalisations in environmental recordings (Briggs et al., 2012).
- Automated classification techniques offer a promising approach for efficient bird species analysis with a large
- number of environmental recordings. However, acquiring a labelled dataset for statistical model generation is
- sometimes laborious and expensive. For bird species richness surveys, one might not be able to know what
- species are in the recordings beforehand. It is indeed our purpose to develop an automated approach to
- ameliorate the difficulty in manually searching for bird species.

#### **1.2 Non-negative matrix factorisation**

- _Non-negative matrix factorisation_ (Lee and Seung, 1999) can decompose a matrix into a product of two
- matrices. The felicity of such decomposition is it enables to generate a parts-based representation, enabling to
- characterise distinct bird vocalisations of a spectrogram. Since its inception, non-negative matrix factorisation
- has seen a broad range of applications, including multiple sound sources separation (Smaragdis, 2004; Zhang
- et al., 2008), music transcription (Bertin et al., 2007), and gene data expression (Frigyesi and Höglund, 2008;
- Hutchins et al., 2008). Recently, probabilistic latent component analysis (PLCA) a probabilistic variant of
- non-negative matrix factorisation has been proposed for the analysis of soundscape ecology (Eldridge A. C.,
- 2016). This paper also motivates our work.
- Non-negative matrix factorisation is described as follows. Given a matrix _S_ of size _n_ × _m_, it can be
- represented by the multiplication of two non-negative matrices _W_ and _H_:

$$S \approx W \cdot H \tag{1}$$

- where the matrix _W_ has a size of _n_ × _r_ and the matrix _H_ has a size of _r_ × _m_. Here, _r_ is called the _factorisation rank_, affecting the performance of the approximation.
- The approximation is achieved by minimizing a cost function that measures approximation error. One of the cost functions is:

$$D = \frac{\|\mathbf{S} - \mathbf{W} \cdot \mathbf{H}\|_{\mathrm{F}}}{\sqrt{n \times m}} \tag{2}$$

where _D_ is the _root-mean-squared (RMS) residual_. The subscript 'F' denotes the _Frobenius_ norm. Let _aij_ be an element of matrix _S_ – _W_ · _H_, the _Frobenius_ norm is calculated as:

86
$$\|\mathbf{S} - \mathbf{W} \cdot \mathbf{H}\|_{F} = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} \sum_{j=1}^{m} |a_{ij}|^{2}}$$
(3)

The algorithm is iterative starting with random initial values for matrices W and H. During the iteration, the

matrices W and H are updated using the following equations:

89
$$\mathbf{W}_{iq} \leftarrow \mathbf{W}_{iq} \cdot \frac{(\mathbf{S} \cdot \mathbf{H}^T)_{iq}}{(\mathbf{W} \cdot (\mathbf{H} \cdot \mathbf{H}^T))_{iq}} \quad for \ 1 \le i \le n \ and \ 1 \le q \le r$$
(4)

90
$$H_{qj} \leftarrow H_{qj} \cdot \frac{(\mathbf{W}^T \cdot \mathbf{S})_{qj}}{\left((\mathbf{W}^T \cdot \mathbf{W}) \cdot \mathbf{H}\right)_{qj}} \quad for \ 1 \le q \le r \ and \ 1 \le j \le m$$
(5)

- The key parameter in equations (4) and (5) is factorisation rank r, which determines the size of matrices W
- and _H_. Other variants of non-negative matrix factorisation algorithm differ in the non-negativity constraints
- on the matrix _W_, the matrix _H_, or both (Hoyer, 2004; Pascual-Montano et al., 2006; Tao et al., 2002).
- A simple example of non-negative matrix factorisation on a spectrogram can be found in this paper
- 95 (Smaragdis, 2004). Generally, the columns of the matrix W denote the distinct spectral profiles and the rows
- of the matrix $\mathbf{H}$ denote the corresponding temporal coefficients of each spectral profile. The primary
- 97 contributions of this paper include the use of non-negative matrix factorisation to extract distinct bird
- 98 vocalisations from environmental recordings with non-targeted multiple species inventories; and a greedy
- algorithm that samples audio clips with the most new distinct bird vocalisations.

#### 100 2 Methods

106

- The general process of our method is described as follows. First, we apply the non-negative matrix
- factorisation to decompose spectrograms into spectral profile matrices and temporal coefficient matrices. A
- hierarchical clustering technique is then used to generate distinct spectra of bird vocalisations from the
- decomposed matrices. Finally, audio clips are sampled in a sequence that offers the maximum number of new
- distinct spectral profiles.

#### 2.1 Estimating the factorisation rank

- The most crucial issue in this study is to determine a proper factorisation rank _r_ for the non-negative matrix
- factorisation. There is no uniform r for the non-negative matrix factorisation due to the inherent complexity of
- environmental recordings. A common solution is to optimise the factorisation performance by increasing r
- 110 (Brunet et al., 2004; Hutchins et al., 2008).
- This work follows an adaptive method proposed by Frigyesi and Höglund (Frigyesi and Höglund, 2008) to
- determine the factorisation rank r based on the acoustic complexity of each recording. For two consecutive
- factorisation rank r-1 and r, we calculate the decreases of root-mean-squared (RMS) residual for the original
- spectrogram ( $\Delta D_0$ ) and its randomised counterpart ( $\Delta D_{random}$ ). If $\Delta D_0 > \Delta D_{random}$ , then there is additional
- information yet to be captured and a larger r is required; if $\Delta D_0 \le \Delta D_{random}$ , then any increase of r will only
- capture noise. The last r that has the $\Delta D_o > \Delta D_{random}$ is considered as a proper factorisation rank for a specific
- spectrogram. In Frigyesi and Höglund's work, the spectrogram is randomised across the rows of each column,
- which might be appropriate for gene analysis. By contrast, we randomise both the rows (frequencies) and
- 119 columns (time frames) of a spectrogram since bird vocalisations could be broadband and last for several time
- 120 frames.

124

- 121 Considering the RMS residual may converge to local minima, we repeat the non-negative matrix factorisation
- 122 30 times with random initial values for each spectrogram and select the ones with the smallest residual as the
- results. We use the NMF package in R to implement the non-negative matrix factorisation.

#### 2.2 Extracting distinct spectra of bird vocalisations

- Non-negative matrix factorisation of a spectrogram results in a spectral profile matrix W and a temporal
- coefficient matrix **H**. A column in matrix **W** represents a distinct spectrum and its corresponding row in

- matrix _H_ represents the temporal position of that spectrum. When multiplied, they approximate to a specific
- spectrum occurring repeatedly in a spectrogram (Smaragdis and Brown, 2003). For an in-field recording, it is
- not uncommon to find some bird vocalisations consisting of non-consecutive harmonies. One should be aware
- that non-negative matrix factorisation does not detect exact bird vocalisations, but rather unique acoustic
- events. Consequently, these vocalisations might be partitioned into small spectral profiles. It is necessary to
- integrate them into a compact spectrum of a bird vocalisation.
- Spectral profiles of the same vocalisation should occur simultaneously. Based upon the acoustic niche
- hypothesis (Farina, 2014), different bird vocalisations should partition in time and frequency to reduce inter-
- specific competitions. Therefore, the temporal coefficients can be used to address this problem. We use a
- hierarchical clustering technique to group similar temporal coefficients of matrix _H_ into different categories,
- yielding a clustered temporal coefficient matrix \*H\*\* . The corresponding spectral profiles that fall into the same
- category are then averaged, resulting in a clustered spectral profile matrix \*W\*\* . The clustered spectral profiles
- are later considered as distinct spectra of bird vocalisations to represent unique bird species.
- Determining a proper number of clusters is crucial for the hierarchical clustering technique. Again, due to the
- complex acoustic contents, there is no fixed threshold for various spectrograms of one-minute audio clips. In
- this study, a dynamic tree cut package in R (Langfelder et al., 2008) is used for the adaptive determination of a
- proper number of temporal coefficient clusters, and hence spectral profile clusters.

# **2.3 Sampling audio clips**

- A codebook of distinct spectra of bird vocalisations is created using the clustered spectral profiles. This
- codebook reflects all unique types of bird vocalisations within the target 605 one-minute audio clips. The
- initial step of creating a codebook is to select an arbitrary audio clip and consider the clustered spectral
- profiles as unique bird vocalisations. Then, an exhaustive search is conducted over the rest of the audio clips,
- adding dissimilar spectral profiles into the codebook as new bird vocalisations. The similarity between two
- spectral profiles is measured by the Pearson's correlation coefficient. Any correlation that is smaller than 0.7
- is considered to be dissimilar because it is widely accepted threshold for determining similarity between two
- vectors.
- Given this codebook of spectral profiles, we now know which type of bird vocalisation in each audio clip. By
- assuming that no species share similar vocalisations and any species has a low diversity of vocalisations, we
- further use these spectral profiles to indicate distinct bird species in the recordings. A greedy algorithm is
- developed to sample audio clips so that each audio clip can provide the maximum number of new spectral
- profiles, which might reflect new bird species. The algorithm is described as follows:
- 1. Identify present/absent information of spectral profiles in each audio clip using the codebook;
- 2. Find the audio clip that contains the most spectral profiles. If there is a tie, select an arbitrary one;
- 3. Search through the remaining audio clips and select the audio clip that provides the most new spectral profiles based on the selected audio clips. If there is a tie, select an arbitrary one;
- 4. Go back to step 3 until no new spectral profile can be found.
- By listening to these sampled audio clips, one is expected to identify the maximum number of bird species
- while listening to the minimum number of one-minute audio clips.

### **2.4 Dataset**

- The research area is called the Samford Ecological Research Facility, located in Brisbane, Australia (27.39˚S,
- 152.88˚E). All recordings were recorded with a sampling rate of 22050 Hz, 16 bits in stereo. They were later
- downsampled to 17640 Hz under the assumption that no bird vocalisations of interest were above 8820 Hz.
- The recordings used in this study were collected on 15th October 2010 when there was intermittent rain and
- wind. Additionally, one-minute audio clips were split and given to three experienced bird observers to
- annotate the present or absent bird species (Wimmer et al., 2013). These annotations are used to validate the

efficiency of bird species surveys. Due to the limitation of bird annotations at a one-minute resolution, the recordings are also cut into one minute duration, resulting in 1435 one-minute audio clips.

Finding an audio clip containing no bird vocalisations is a commonplace. Consider, for example, rainy and windy periods. Obviously, these audio clips make no contributions to bird species surveys. In a previous research, we had created a classification model with an accuracy of 82.4% to remove the redundant recordings that are unlikely to contain bird species. It resulted in 605 out of 1435 (42%) one-minute audio clips and 59 out of 62 (95%) bird species (Zhang et al., 2015). We also applied a high-pass filter to eliminate region-specific low-frequency noise below 1000 Hz. The analysis is conducted using R 3.0.2 and RStudio 0.98.994.

#### **3.2 Generation of distinct spectral profiles**

Using the decrease of RMS residual normally leads to a large factorisation rank _r_ which could exceed the actual number of bird vocalisations. Although a large _r_ provides enough expressive power to represent the original spectrogram, it also divides a spectrogram into small spectral segments. We cannot use these spectral profiles as distinct bird vocalisations since some vocalisations are separated into fractional components.

As mentioned in section 2.2, a hierarchical clustering technique has been used to group the fractional components that belong to the same vocalisation together. Figure 2 displays the reconstructed spectrogram of the 398th minute using the clustering technique. Compared to the reconstructed spectrogram with a factorisation rank _r_ of 28 (Figure 1: bottom), the majority of different types of bird vocalisations are recovered in Figure 2. It is not surprising to find that vocalisation No. 3 is absent because the initial factorisation does not capture this acoustic event. The clustering technique also introduces some artefacts to both the clustered spectral profile matrix \*W\** and clustered temporal coefficient matrix *H\*\* , which blurred the reconstructed spectrogram.

To further understand subtle details, we plot all 9 spectral profiles of matrix \*W\*\* in Figure 3. One can verify which clustered spectral profiles contribute to which type of vocalisation in the reconstructed spectrogram in Figure 2. For example, W1 and W2 register vocalisation No. 1; W3 covers the high frequency component of vocalisation No.2; W4 contributes to vocalisation No. 4; W5 is a combination of vocalisations No. 2 and No. 5 because they occupy a similar frequency range; W6 and W7 divide vocalisation No. 6 into high and low frequency components; finally, W8 and W9 capture vocalisations No. 7 and No. 8 respectively.

# **3.3 Sampling audio clips to assist bird species surveys**

The hierarchical clustering and dynamic tree pruning are applied to the target 605 one-minute audio clips, resulting in a list of clustered spectral profiles for each audio clip. Using the similarity measure described in section 3.3, we create a codebook of distinct bird vocalisations using the clustered spectral profiles and use it to identify present bird vocalisations in each audio clip. Our greedy algorithm is later implemented to sample audio clips so that each audio clip provides the maximum number of new distinct bird vocalisations.

Species accumulation curves are plotted in Figure 4, demonstrating the efficiency of bird species richness surveys in a one-day recording. The figure can be interpreted as the percent of bird species found (y axis) in the given number of one-minute audio clips (x axis). Two benchmarks have been drawn. The triangular denotes the highest efficiency of determining unique bird species with ground-truth annotations; whereas the green circle serves as a non-informative strategy that samples 1000 times at random from 1435 one-minute audio clips. Any useful strategy should reside between these two curves. The orange circle is the currently best published strategy called _dawn sampling_ (Wimmer et al., 2013), which selects audio clips during three hours after dawn. Here, we randomise the process 1000 times and use the average. The first 120 one-minute audio clips of different species accumulation curves are drawn to compare their efficiency.

It can be seen from Figure 4 that the efficiency of surveying birds using non-negative matrix factorisation method does not exceed that of dawn sampling until 75 one-minute audio clip samples. Particularly in the first 60 one-minute audio clip sample, the non-negative matrix factorisation method (71.0%) finds a comparable percent of bird species as the dawn sampling (72.6%). Note that the species accumulation curve of dawn sampling increases rapidly but also exhausts quickly because of the species that do not vocalise at dawn. By contrast, the curve generated by the non-negative matrix factorisation method (Figure 4: red squares) is subject to low efficiency in the beginning but rises quickly after 75 samples. The reason for these differences lies in the total number of audio clip candidates being sampled. Dawn sampling is based on prior knowledge that there is a high density of bird vocalisations during three hours after dawn, but such a method will miss species that vocalise at other times. The proposed method does not have these time constraints and can search

exhaustively from audio clip samples that are likely to contain bird species. Consequently, the proposed method enables to find 56 out of 62 (90.3%) bird species in 120 one-minute audio clip samples.

To capitalise on both methods, the first 120 one-minute audio clips sampled by the proposed method are selected and those during the dawn are moved to the beginning of the sampled sequence while keeping their relative order. The species accumulation curve (Figure 4: blue squares) demonstrates that dawn sampling compensates the proposed method for the inefficiency in the beginning. The combination of the non-negative matrix factorisation-based method and the dawn sampling outperforms either method used alone.

# **4 Discussion and conclusion**

The use of non-negative matrix factorisation has two niceties of decomposing a spectrogram into a spectral profile matrix and a temporal coefficient matrix. One attraction is the spectral profiles can be considered as indicators of distinct bird vocalisations and utilised to sample audio clips. This method assists people in conducting efficient bird species richness surveys. The other felicity is the temporal coefficients indicate the

- occurrences of the corresponding spectral profiles, which might merit further investigation of the abundance
- of vocalisations.
- The limitations of this technique are two-fold. First, we assume that there are no sharing vocalisations of
- different species and any species has a low diversity of vocalisations. In our recordings, there is a total of 62
- bird species and the majority (85.5%) have no more than two types of vocalisations. Those with one
- vocalisation and with two vocalisations comprise 50% and 35.5% respectively. The remaining 14.5% species
- have 3 to 5 types of vocalisations. Second, the proposed method is only implemented on a one-day in-field
- recording collected from a sub-tropical area. Future work is needed on recordings that cover a large
- spatiotemporal scale.
- The non-negative matrix factorisation method has a promising application in analysis of environmental
- recordings with non-targeted multiple species inventories. Although we focus on birds, the proposed method
- should be applicable to analysis of other vocal species such as crickets and frogs. This work also broadens the
- scope of non-negative matrix factorisation from gene expression and image representation to include faunal
- vocalisation detection.
